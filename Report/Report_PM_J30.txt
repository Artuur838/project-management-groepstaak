2. RESEARCH QUESTION AND DESIGN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

2.1 Main research question

To what extent does the performance of priority rules for the
resource-constrained project scheduling problem depend on the project
characteristics network complexity (NC), resource factor (RF) and resource
strength (RS), and can an improved heuristic increase that performance,
especially for the hardest projects?

Part A of the question is descriptive: we measure how far 13 standard
priority rules, each combined with two schedule generation schemes (SGS),
stay from the known optimal project duration on the 480 J30 projects, and
how this deviation changes with NC, RF and RS. Part B is design-oriented:
we use the weaknesses found in part A to build an improved heuristic and
measure how much of the remaining gap it closes and at what cost in
computation time.

2.2 Sub-questions

Part A - the existing priority rules
  A1. How large is the average deviation of each rule/SGS combination from
      the optimal makespan, and for how many projects does each one find
      the optimum?
  A2. Which project parameter (NC, RF or RS) has the largest influence on
      that deviation?
  A3. Do the best rule and the best SGS (serial or parallel) change
      depending on the parameter values?
  A4. How much of the extension of the project beyond its CPM duration is
      caused by resource scarcity itself (unavoidable), and how much by the
      choice of the rule (avoidable)?

Part B - our improvement
  B1. Does our improvement reduce the average deviation from the optimum,
      and how many projects does it solve to optimality?
  B2. Is the gain concentrated in the hardest projects (low RS, high RF)?
  B3. How much extra computation time does it cost, compared with a single
      priority rule and with the optimal method of experiment 4?

2.3 Hypotheses

The hypotheses follow from the definition of the parameters. A low RS means
that the resources are scarce compared with the peak demand, so more
activities compete for the same resources and the order in which a rule
schedules them matters more (H1). A high RF means that each activity uses
more resource types, which creates more conflicts (H2). NC only changes the
precedence network and is expected to matter less (H3). At RS = 1.0 the
availability equals the peak demand of the earliest-start schedule, so in
theory the resources never bind (H4). H5 and H6 are the matching
expectations for part B.

Table 2 - Hypotheses
+----+----------------------------------------------------------------------+
| #  | Hypothesis                                                           |
+----+----------------------------------------------------------------------+
| H1 | The lower RS, the larger the deviation of all rules from the optimum.|
| H2 | A higher RF increases the deviation.                                 |
| H3 | NC has less influence than RS and RF.                                |
| H4 | At RS = 1.0, every rule finds the optimal makespan.                  |
| H5 | Our improvement reduces the deviation most at low RS.                |
| H6 | At high RS, the improvement yields almost nothing.                   |
+----+----------------------------------------------------------------------+

2.4 Experimental design and assumptions

Full factorial design. Every one of the 480 J30 projects is scheduled with
every combination of 13 priority rules and 2 schedule generation schemes,
which gives 26 combinations and 12,480 schedules in total.

Priority rules (as implemented in ProPy, experiment 3), in four groups:
  - Activity rules:   SPT (shortest processing time), LPT (longest
                      processing time)
  - Network rules:    MIS (most immediate successors), MTS (most total
                      successors), LNRJ (least non-related jobs), GRPW
                      (greatest rank positional weight)
  - CPM-based rules:  EST, EFT (earliest start / finish time), LST, LFT
                      (latest start / finish time), MSLK (minimum slack)
  - Resource rules:   GRWC (greatest resource work content), GCRWC
                      (greatest cumulative resource work content)

Schedule generation schemes:
  - Serial SGS: takes the activities one by one in priority order and
    places each one at its earliest precedence- and resource-feasible
    start time. It produces active schedules.
  - Parallel SGS: moves forward in time and, at each decision point,
    starts as many eligible activities as the resources allow, in priority
    order. It produces non-delay schedules.

Performance measures (computed per project and then averaged):
  - Deviation from the optimum (%) =
        (makespan - optimal makespan) / optimal makespan x 100
  - % projects optimal: share of projects where the makespan equals the
    optimum.
  - Average rank: per project the 26 combinations are ranked on makespan
    (1 = best, ties get the average rank), then averaged over the projects.
  - Best-of-26: per project the shortest makespan over all 26
    combinations. It is the benchmark for "choose the best rule
    afterwards" and the starting point for part B.
  - Decomposition (section 4.4): the extension of the makespan beyond the
    CPM duration, split into CPM -> optimum and optimum -> rule, both
    expressed as % of the CPM duration so that the two parts add up.

The 120 projects with RS = 1.0 are reported separately. Section 4.2 shows
that the resources never bind there and every combination is optimal.
Including them would make every rule look better without saying anything
about the rules. Unless stated otherwise, the averages in tables 3 and 5
are therefore over the remaining 360 projects (RS < 1.0). For the
parameter analysis we also report eta squared (the share of the variance
in the deviation explained by one parameter) as a simple effect size.

Assumptions:
  - Deterministic activity durations, known in advance.
  - No pre-emption: an activity that has started runs until it finishes.
  - Four renewable resources with a constant availability over time.
  - The published optima in j30opt.sm (Demeulemeester & Herroelen) are
    correct. As a check, for every project and every rule we verified
    CPM duration <= optimum <= rule makespan.
  - Ties in priority are broken by the lowest activity number, the order
    in the project file. This makes the experiment fully deterministic
    (one run per combination, no random seed involved).

3.1 What ProPy already provided

ProPy (Vanhoucke) provides the building blocks for a single project:
  - Experiment 3 (my_Experiment3_heuristic.py): reading a project in
    Patterson (.rcp) format, computing the CPM schedule and the resource
    scarcity indicators, building a priority list with one of the 13
    priority rules (create_priority_list), and building a feasible schedule
    with the serial or parallel SGS (serial_sgs, parallel_sgs in
    my_RCP_Scheduler.py), including Gantt charts and resource profiles.
  - Experiment 4 (my_Experiment4_optimal.py): solving the RCPSP to
    optimality with an integer programming model in PuLP/CBC. This only
    works for small projects within a reasonable time.
  - The module my_RCP_Scheduler.py also contains backward versions of both
    SGSs (backward_serial_sgs, backward_parallel_sgs). Experiment 3 does
    not use them, but they are the basis of our improvement (3.3).

ProPy works on one project at a time, reads only the .rcp format, and
compares a schedule only with the CPM duration, not with the optimum.

3.2 What we added

  1. Conversion PSPLIB .sm -> .rcp (convert_sm_to_rcp.py). PSPLIB publishes
     J30 in its own .sm format. The script reads precedences, durations,
     resource requests and availabilities, and writes all 480 files to
     Example_Files/j30_rcp so that ProPy can read them unchanged.
  2. Batch experiment over all 480 projects
     (Python_Experiments/my_Experiment3_j30_study.py). For each project it
     computes the CPM duration and the makespan of the 26 rule/SGS
     combinations, and stores everything in j30_all_results.csv (12,480
     rows) plus a summary per combination.
  3. Fixed tie-break. With equal priority values, the activity with the
     lowest number is chosen. This makes the results reproducible.
  4. Linking with the project parameters and the optimum
     (Python_Experiments/my_Experiment3_j30_analysis.py):
       - NC, RF and RS are derived from the parameter number p in the file
         name j30p_i: p = 1-16 -> NC 1.5, 17-32 -> NC 1.8, 33-48 -> NC 2.1;
         within each block RF increases every 4 values (0.25, 0.50, 0.75,
         1.00) and RS cycles 0.2, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0. We checked this mapping by
         recomputing NC and RF directly from the .sm files.
       - The optimal makespans are read from j30opt.sm (PSPLIB).
  5. Automatic tables and figures: all tables and figures in section 4 are
     generated by the analysis script.
  6. Our improvement: forward-backward improvement (justification) on top
     of the best-of-26 schedule. [TO CONFIRM once part B is implemented]

3.3 Our improvement in detail
[TO CONFIRM - draft based on the planned method; update once implemented]

The results of part A (section 4) point to two weaknesses. First, no
single rule is best for all projects. Choosing the best of the 26
combinations per project already lowers the average deviation from 5.69%
(best single rule) to 2.47%. Second, even that best-of-26 schedule is still
4.95% above the optimum at RS = 0.2, and is optimal in only 20% of those
projects. Priority rules make one pass and never revise a decision. The
remaining gap is therefore caused by early choices that turn out badly
later in the schedule.

Forward-backward improvement (also called justification; Valls et al.,
2005) repairs exactly this kind of mistake without a new rule:

  1. Start from a feasible schedule S (here: the best of the 26
     combinations for the project).
  2. Backward pass: sort the activities on decreasing finish time in S and
     schedule them with the backward serial SGS, so each activity is
     shifted as far right as possible (right justification).
  3. Forward pass: sort the activities on increasing start time in the
     right-justified schedule and schedule them again with the forward
     serial SGS, so each activity is shifted as far left as possible (left
     justification).
  4. If the makespan improved, set S to the new schedule and go back to
     step 2. Otherwise stop.

Each pass keeps the schedule feasible and can never make the makespan
longer, so the method always ends with a schedule at least as good as the
starting schedule. Because it only reuses ProPy's own SGS functions, the
extra computation time stays a few extra SGS passes per project, far
below the integer programming model of experiment 4.

Conclusion for table 3:
LFT with the parallel SGS is the best single combination: on average 5.69%
above the optimum, with the best average rank (7.3 out of 26). The top five
consists entirely of rules that use the latest start or finish times (LFT,
LST) or the total number of successors (MTS). These rules look ahead in the
network and give priority to activities with little room to slip. Simple
activity rules perform worst: SPT with the serial SGS is 22.8% above the
optimum on average. Remarkably, the two rules that explicitly look at
resource use (GRWC, GCRWC) only end up in the middle and lower part of the
ranking (11-16%).

The parallel SGS gives a lower average deviation than the serial SGS for
all 13 rules (9.9% vs. 13.3% averaged over the rules). The serial SGS,
however, more often finds the exact optimum: LST-Serial is optimal in 35%
of the projects, against 25% for LST-Parallel. This matches the theory
(Kolisch, 1996). The serial SGS searches among active schedules, a set that
always contains an optimal schedule, but it has more large outliers. The
parallel SGS searches among non-delay schedules, which sometimes excludes
the optimum but is more robust on average.

Even the best rule can be far off for an individual project: up to 27.6%
above the optimum. No single rule dominates. If the best of the 26
combinations is chosen per project, the average deviation drops from 5.69%
to 2.47%, and the share of optimally solved projects rises from 23.9% to
51.9%. This is the baseline for our improvement in part B.

Conclusion for figures 1 and 2:
H1 (lower RS -> larger deviation): confirmed. RS is the parameter with by
far the largest effect. For the best rule, the deviation falls from 9.1% at
RS = 0.2 to 4.8% at 0.5, 3.2% at 0.7 and 0% at 1.0. For the average rule it
falls from 17.0% to 0%. RS alone explains 40-67% of the variance in the
deviation. The heatmap shows the same pattern in every RF row. There is
one small exception (RF = 0.25: 1.6% at RS 0.5 vs. 1.8% at RS 0.7), which
falls within the noise of 30 projects per cell.

H2 (higher RF -> larger deviation): largely confirmed. The deviation of the
best rule rises sharply from 2.5% (RF 0.25) to 6.4% (RF 0.50), but then
levels off (7.0% at 0.75, 6.9% at 1.00). The effect is therefore not
linear: once activities use at least half of the resource types, adding
more types hardly makes the scheduling decisions harder for the rule. The
heatmap shows that RF matters most in combination with scarce resources.
At RS = 0.2 the deviation rises from 4.1% (RF 0.25) to 10-12% (RF >= 0.5).

H3 (NC matters less than RS and RF): confirmed. NC explains only 1-2% of
the variance. The small effect it has goes in the opposite direction to
what one might expect: denser networks give a slightly lower deviation
(6.3% -> 5.0% for the best rule). A plausible explanation is that more
precedence relations leave fewer possible orders of the activities, so a
rule has fewer chances to make a wrong choice.

H4 (at RS = 1.0 every rule is optimal): confirmed. For all 120 projects
with RS = 1.0, all 26 combinations find the optimal makespan, and that
optimum equals the CPM duration. This follows from the definition of RS:
at RS = 1.0 the availability equals the peak demand of the earliest-start
schedule, so the resources never bind and the choice of rule does not
matter.

same family of latest-time rules (LFT, LST). The best SGS changes as well.

to the strongest rules only: combined with a good priority list, the
serial SGS can use its larger search space when resources are less tight.
This is in line with Kolisch (1996), who reports that neither SGS
dominates and that their relative performance depends on the instance
characteristics, with the parallel SGS strongest for single-pass priority

LFT-Parallel is the best for all three NC values. For a project manager
this means that RS (and to a lesser extent RF) is enough to choose a rule:
LFT-Parallel when resources are scarce, LST-Serial when they are fairly
ample.

The makespan of a resource-feasible schedule is longer than the CPM
duration for two reasons. The first part, from the CPM duration to the
optimum, is caused by the resource scarcity itself. No scheduling method
can avoid it. The second part, from the optimum to the makespan of the
rule, is caused by the rule and can in principle be improved. (Here both
parts are expressed as % of the CPM duration, so they add up. That is why
the rule part differs slightly from the deviations in table 3, which are
relative to the optimum.)

When resources are scarce (RS 0.2), even the optimal schedule is on
average 44% longer than the CPM plan. A plan that ignores resources is
therefore far too optimistic for such projects. The best rule adds another
13.7% on top of that. That is the largest absolute loss, but only about a
quarter of the total extension. As resources become more ample, the
unavoidable part shrinks much faster than the rule part. At RS 0.7 more
than half of the (small) extension comes from the rule. Over all projects
with RS < 1.0, about 29% of the extension beyond CPM is caused by the rule
(7.4% of the CPM duration) and 71% by the scarcity itself.

For part B this means that the largest absolute gain can be made at low
RS, where the rule adds 13.7% (best rule) to 25% (average rule) of the
CPM duration. This is the motivation for H5.

5.1 Comparison of the methods

We compare our improvement (forward-backward improvement with the serial
SGS, "FBI serial") with the same procedure built on the parallel SGS ("FBI
parallel"). The parallel variant serves as a control. It has no guarantee
that a pass never lengthens the schedule (section 3.3), so a new schedule
is only accepted when it is strictly shorter. Both variants start from the
best of the 26 schedules. As an extra variant, we apply each FBI to all 26
schedules of a project and keep the best result.

Conclusion for table 5:
FBI serial lowers the average deviation of the best-of-26 schedule from
2.47% to 1.98%, and raises the share of optimally solved projects from
51.9% to 57.2%. It improves the schedule in 63 of the 360 projects and never
makes it worse. Compared with the best single rule (5.69%), about two
thirds of the gap to the optimum is closed. The extra cost is small: on
average 3 ms per project on top of the 66 ms for the 26 schedules. The
method also converges fast: in no project did a second iteration bring a
further improvement.

The parallel control performs worse on average (2.09%, 54.2% optimal). It
improves 41 projects instead of 63. In a direct comparison the serial
variant gives the shorter schedule in 40 projects, the parallel variant in
21, and in 299 projects they are equal. The control also confirms the
theory of section 3.3. Not one serial pass lengthened a schedule, in all
480 projects. Parallel passes lengthened the schedule in 262 of the 360
projects at least once. In 156 projects the schedule after the first
parallel iteration was even longer than the starting schedule S. Without
the acceptance check, the parallel variant would therefore often end up
worse than where it started.

The largest gain comes from the extra variant. Applying FBI serial to all
26 schedules and keeping the best halves the deviation of the best of 26
(2.47% -> 1.17%) and solves 67.2% of the projects to optimality, at about
0.19 s per project. A schedule that was not the best of the 26 can become
the best after justification. Here too, serial beats parallel (1.17% vs.
1.24%, 67.2% vs. 60.3% optimal).

Conclusion (test H5 and H6):
H5 (the improvement helps most at low RS): confirmed. The gain of FBI
serial is largest at RS = 0.2 (0.82 percentage points, 29% of the projects
improved) and falls to 0.53 at RS 0.5 and 0.12 at RS 0.7. RF shows the same
picture from the other side: the more resource types an activity uses, the
larger the gain (0.16 at RF 0.25 up to 0.88 at RF 1.0). FBI therefore helps
exactly where part A found the largest gap (sections 4.2 and 4.4): scarce
resources and many resource conflicts. Even there, a gap remains. At
RS 0.2 the deviation after FBI is still 4.1%, and only 24% of those
projects are solved to optimality.

H6 (almost no gain at high RS): confirmed. At RS 0.7 the gain is only 0.12
percentage points. The best of 26 is already within 0.5% of the optimum
there, and optimal in 85% of the projects, so there is little left to
improve. At RS 1.0 there is nothing to improve at all (section 4.2).

Serial vs. parallel per project type: the serial variant is clearly better
at moderate scarcity (RS 0.5: gain 0.53 vs. 0.20) and at RF 0.25-0.50. When
resources are very scarce (RS 0.2) and at RF 0.75, the parallel variant
gains slightly more (0.89 vs. 0.82 at RS 0.2). A plausible explanation is
that with very scarce resources, a non-delay schedule is more often close to
optimal, so the parallel SGS's tendency to start activities as early as
possible does less harm. This difference is small compared with the
overall advantage of the serial variant and with its guarantee of never
lengthening a schedule. For that reason the serial variant remains our
improvement.
